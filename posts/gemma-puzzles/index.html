Teaching Gemma 1B made-up puzzles with reinforcement learning

Three puzzle families, a string-match checker, and 45 minutes of GRPO on one TPU chip.

Part of Teaching Gemma 1B, a series of small post-training experiments on one 1B model.

On held-out test puzzles, GRPO took Gemma 3 1B from 33–59% to 96–100% on the trained puzzle families, and from 34–60% to 93–97% on harder variants it never trained on. An untrained control task did not move. The one failure is instructive: a variant the base model solved 0.6% of the time stayed at 0%, because RL can only reinforce what the model sometimes gets right.

Why RL, and why a 1B model

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards is the post-training step behind most recent reasoning gains. The model samples its own answers, a program grades them, and the model is pushed toward the answers that scored well. No human labels or teacher model are needed, only a checker.

A 1B model makes the loop cheap enough to iterate on. One GRPO step on Gemma 3 1B takes about 15 seconds on a single TPU v6e chip, so a full run costs a few dollars and finishes in under an hour.

The task: made-up puzzle families

Every puzzle comes from a short Python generator and has exactly one correct answer, so the reward is a string match. Each prompt ends with “Think it through, then end your reply with a line of the form ‘Answer: ...’”. A missing or malformed answer line scores 0.

Training draws fresh puzzles from random seeds; dev (200 per set) and test (500 per set) use fixed, disjoint seed ranges. Arithmetic was left out of training because the base model already scores about 95% on it.

Setup: GRPO in one JAX process

The loop is about 200 lines of JAX on top of Tunix (its Gemma 3 model and KV-cache sampler) with LoRA adapters. Sampling and training share one set of weights in one process, so nothing is copied to a separate inference server. Each step:

  1. Draw 64 fresh puzzles and sample 8 answers to each at temperature 1.0.
  2. Score each answer 1 or 0.
  3. Compute group-relative advantages: reward minus the group mean, divided by the group's standard deviation. Groups where all 8 answers agree carry no signal and are dropped.
  4. Take one policy-gradient step weighted by advantage. The loss is divided by a constant rather than each answer's length, which avoids rewarding long wrong answers.

Gemma 3 1B instruction-tuned · LoRA rank 32, alpha 64 · AdamW lr 1e-5, grad clip 1.0, no KL penalty · 1 × TPU v6e · ~15 s per step · 250 steps.

What I learned

  • The first run collapsed, and effective batch size was the cause. With lr 3e-5 and 32 prompts per step, binary reached 68% by step 50 and then fell to 7–30%. As the model improved, most groups became all-right or all-wrong and were dropped, leaving only 16–24 rollouts with any signal, and Adam still took a full-size step on that noisy gradient. Halving the learning rate and doubling the prompts gave a smooth climb.
  • The learning signal dries up on its own. By step 200, only 2–8 of 512 rollouts per step had a non-zero advantage. Further progress needs harder prompts, such as a curriculum.
  • Reward needs some successes to work with. Hard binary started at 0.6% and ended at 0%. Check best-of-k at baseline, not single-try accuracy, before spending compute.
  • No answer-length blow-up. Mean reply length stayed between 150 and 180 tokens.

Total spend about $57, mostly idle TPU time between runs; the successful run itself cost about $2.25.
