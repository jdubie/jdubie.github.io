Teaching Gemma 1B to count letters

“How many r's are in strawberry?” is the classic question small language models get wrong. Four thousand synthetic examples and a few minutes of fine-tuning take Google's smallest Gemma from 20% to 84% on words it has never seen.

Part of Teaching Gemma 1B, a series of small post-training experiments on one 1B model.

Base Gemma 3 1B counted letters correctly 20% of the time. After supervised fine-tuning on examples that spell each word out letter by letter, it gets 84% right on words it never saw in training. Training on the same questions with only the final number gets 49%. The model didn't need new knowledge, just a procedure it can follow.

Why a 1B model can't count letters

Language models read tokens, not letters. To Gemma, “wellness” is one or two tokens, so “how many e's?” asks about something it never directly sees. Asked that question, base Gemma answered “The letter ‘e’ appears 3 times in the word ‘wellness’.” The answer is 2.

It can't reliably spell, either. Asked to spell 100 held-out words one letter at a time, base Gemma got 22% of attempts exactly right: “children” came back as ch-i-r-o-n-s, “maintenance” as en-ti-fee. So fine-tuning has to teach two things: write the word out letter by letter, then count along the way. Once the letters are on the page, counting is easy.

The data costs nothing

Every training example is generated by a few lines of Python. I took 7,048 common English words, held out every fifth one for testing (1,762 words the model never trains on), and for each example picked a word and a letter so that the answers 0, 1, 2 and 3 are equally common. That balance matters: in my first version most answers were 1, and a model that always said “1” would have scored 60%. With balanced answers, always guessing the same number scores 25%. The target response spells the word out, marks each match, and states the count:

Spelling out 'gmbh' letter by letter:
1. g
2. m
3. b
4. h <- 'h' #1
The letter 'h' appears 1 time.
Answer: 1

As a control, I trained a second model on the same 4,000 questions with the target being only Answer: 1. Both runs use LoRA (rank 64, all linear layers) on Gemma 3 1B instruct, two epochs, learning rate 1e-4, on one L4 GPU.

Results

On 200 held-out questions (50 for each answer from 0 to 3) with 8 samples each, accuracy went from 20% (95% interval 15%–25%) to 84% (80%–88%) with spelled-out training, and to 49% with answer-only training. Training on answers alone more than doubles accuracy, but look at how: given eight tries, the answer-only model gets 95% of questions right at least once, yet only 49% on a single try. It has learned which counts are plausible and guesses among them. The spelled-out model gets 84% on the first try.

The strawberry problem

The famous question is a different story. Asked “How many r's are in strawberry?” 16 times, base Gemma answered 3 16 of 16 times. It has almost certainly seen this exact question in training. The fine-tuned model got it right 0 of 16 times. It spells s-t-r-a-w-b-e-r-y, writing the double r as a single r, and with it loses one of the three.

That points at the fine-tuned model's real weakness: spelling long words. On test words of eight letters or more it spells the whole word exactly right only about 40% of the time. It still counts correctly about 80% of the time, because most spelling slips miss the letter being counted. Strawberry is unlucky: the slip lands on an r.

What this shows

  • SFT teaches procedures, not just answers. The answer-only model saw exactly the same questions and correct counts. Without the intermediate steps it had nothing to imitate except a guess.
  • Small models fail at things their representation hides. Counting letters isn't hard; seeing letters is. Training the model to write the letters out first turns the question into one it can answer.
  • The procedure generalises, even when the spelling is shaky. Every test word was held out of training. The fine-tuned model spells many of them imperfectly, but it still counts correctly most of the time on words it was never taught.
  • Synthetic data is enough when the answer is checkable. No human labels and no teacher model: a 20-line generator produced every example and the grader for the test.

Sampling at temperature 1.0 throughout. Test words are disjoint from training words. Code and data generators are about 150 lines of Python.
