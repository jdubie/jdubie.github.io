Teaching Gemma 1B Wordle: SFT for the rules, RL for the strategy

Starting from the raw pretrained checkpoint, which has never been taught to follow instructions.

Part of Teaching Gemma 1B, a series of small post-training experiments on one 1B model.

The pretrained Gemma 3 1B won 1 of 500 Wordle games. Supervised fine-tuning on 64,000 games, where each move first writes out what is known so far, took it to 80%. Reinforcement learning on top took it to 85%.

The plan

Wordle splits into two skills. The rules: reply in the right format, guess real five-letter words, and only guess words that fit the colored feedback so far. The strategy: choose guesses that narrow the field fastest. Imitation fits the rules, since there is one right way to read the feedback. Reinforcement learning fits strategy, since the model can learn from its own games what works.

So SFT imitates a simple solver that guesses a random word consistent with everything seen so far: correct, but not clever. Neither the pretrained model nor the instruction-tuned Gemma 3 1B can play at all; given three example games, the instruction-tuned model won 0 of 500, often replying with guesses like “RACE”.

Writing out the state doubles the win rate

Two SFT targets were compared. One is just the guess. The other first writes the current state, then the guess:

Pattern: _ A R _ _
Must include: A (not spot 3), R (not spot 2)
Ruled out: C E M T Y
Guess: PARKA

With the same 4,000 training games, the state-tracking version won twice as often (33% vs 16%). A 1B model has trouble keeping track of earlier feedback silently; writing it down makes each guess an easy step. More games kept helping: 15% at 1,000 games (dev), 33% at 4,000, 58% at 16,000 and 80% at 64,000.

RL: the first run memorized the answers

RL uses GRPO: 32 answer words per step, 8 games each, a reward for winning in fewer guesses (1 + 0.2 for each guess left, minus 0.1 per invalid guess), and a small KL penalty toward the SFT model.

The first run drew its answer words from the same list the SFT data used. Win rate on its training games rose from 72% to 85%, but on held-out dev words it stayed flat (57.5% to 58%): it was learning which words tend to be answers, not how to play. So I split the training words in two. SFT only uses set A (1,015 words), and RL only plays games whose answers are in set B (600 words the model has never seen as an answer). Test words are a third, separate set.

With that split, RL helped on top of both SFT models: from 58.2% to 64.6% after 16k SFT games (+6.4 points, standard error 2.5) and from 80.2% to 85.0% after 64k (+4.8, standard error 2.0), each checkpoint picked on dev after 180 steps. Most of the gain came from more guesses fitting the feedback, not from shorter games: guesses per win barely changed. Rewarding consistent guesses directly, with a bonus on every turn, did worse (61.6%).

What I took away

  • SFT for the rules, then RL, works even from a raw pretrained model. 0% to 80% with SFT, then 85% with RL.
  • Make the model write its state down. Same games, twice the wins.
  • Keep RL prompts away from SFT data. When RL replayed the SFT answer words, it memorized them; on unseen words it generalized.
  • RL here improved reliability more than strategy. The model won more often by making fewer rule mistakes, not by finding the answer in fewer guesses.
