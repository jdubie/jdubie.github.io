Teaching a Unitree G1 not to trip

Reinforcement learning in NVIDIA Isaac Lab for a humanoid walking across a floor littered with 1-inch obstacles, from a stock policy that stumbles to a natural 0.9 Hz stride, plus what sim-to-real realism costs.

Isaac Lab's stock flat-ground G1 policy falls in 5.7% of 20-second walks across a floor scattered with 1-inch (2.54 cm) boxes and discs. A policy trained with PPO on an obstacle curriculum, with a gait clock and rewards for a human-like stride, falls in 0 of 512 walks at 1 inch and in none up to 6 cm. It also slows its feet from a 4.5 Hz shuffle to a 0.89 Hz stride.

Setup

  • Simulator: Isaac Lab 3.0 on Isaac Sim 6.2 (PhysX), 4,096 G1s in parallel on one L4 GPU, about 39,000 simulation steps per second.
  • Policy: an MLP (512-256-128) that reads the robot's own state 50 times a second (body velocity, gravity direction, joint angles and velocities, its last action, the speed command) and outputs target angles for 37 joints. PD controllers track those targets at 200 Hz.
  • Algorithm: PPO from RSL-RL. Each iteration collects 24 steps from every robot (98k transitions), then takes 5 passes of gradient steps over them. 3,000 iterations take about 2 hours.
  • Test: 512 robots per obstacle height, commanded to walk straight at 0.8 m/s (1.8 mph) for 20 seconds with no pushes. A fall is the torso touching the ground.

The obstacle course

Each training tile is a patch of flat floor with 10 to 60 randomly placed boxes and discs. A curriculum sorts tiles into 10 levels, from 1 cm obstacles to 8 cm; a robot that walks far enough is promoted to a harder tile, and one that falls is demoted.

One bug cost an hour. Isaac Lab's repeated-box and repeated-cylinder terrains center each object at floor level, so half of it is buried. The first run trained on obstacles half as tall as configured. I found it by reading the generated mesh heights back out, and now pass twice the height I want visible.

Step 1: a stock policy trips

Isaac Lab ships a flat-ground G1 velocity policy. Trained for 1,500 iterations, it never falls on a bare floor: 512 of 512 robots are still standing after 15 seconds. On clutter it degrades fast: 5.7% falls at 1 inch, 25% at 4 cm, 81% at 6 cm.

Retraining on the obstacle curriculum fixes most of it. I trained two versions: one blind, which only feels the ground through its joints and feet, and one that also gets a 1.6 × 1.0 m height scan of the floor around it (a grid of 693 ray-cast heights, the simulated version of a depth camera's height map).

Both fall rarely, but neither walks like a person. They cross clutter by stamping: 4.5 steps per second per foot, each foot in the air for 0.12 s, feet 31 cm apart, knees bent. Fast short steps are a cheap way to never be caught mid-stride on an obstacle.

Step 2: a natural gait

People walk at about one stride per second. I added a gait clock: the policy sees the sine and cosine of a 0.9 Hz phase, and a reward pays it for matching a contact schedule in which each foot is down 60% of the cycle and the feet are half a cycle apart. Supporting rewards ask for a 9 cm foot lift mid-swing, flat foot landings, a 20 cm step width, a 72 cm torso height, soft touchdowns, and arm swing opposite the same-side leg.

The first version was reward-hacked within 500 iterations. The contact reward paid +1 when a foot's contact matched the schedule and 0 when it didn't. Keeping both feet planted matches the schedule 60% of the time, so the policy shuffled at 3.6 Hz with its feet barely leaving the ground. Version 2 pays −1 for a mismatch, penalizes a foot touching down mid-swing, and rewards air time up to 0.45 s. It settled at 0.89 Hz by iteration 600.

Training curves

The natural-gait run, plotted against PPO iteration (0 to 3,000). Hover a chart for values.

  • The first 50 iterations are pure falling. Every episode ends in a fall within a second, and the curriculum demotes every robot to level 0. Reward dips negative because penalties (torque, joint limits, termination) arrive before any walking does.
  • Standing comes before walking. Episodes go from 2 seconds to 19 between iterations 200 and 400, while speed tracking and the gait match keep improving for another thousand.
  • The curriculum climbs fast once walking works, from level 0 at iteration 300 to 6.6 by iteration 800, then settles near 5.9: robots that clear the hardest level are sent back to a random one. Training always includes the tallest obstacles, so falls never reach zero there.
  • PPO's exploration noise grows from 1.0 to about 1.25 instead of shrinking. The tests above use the mean action, without noise.

Step 3: sim-to-real realism

A policy that only works with perfect motors won't survive real hardware. I retrained with four changes from the sim-to-real literature:

  • Motor delay: each robot gets a random 0–20 ms delay between the policy's command and the motor acting on it.
  • Real torque limits: 88 Nm at the hips and 139 Nm at the knees, the G1's published peaks, instead of Isaac Lab's 300 Nm default.
  • Randomized motors: stiffness and damping ±20%, extra joint friction and armature.
  • Shifted mass: the torso's center of mass moved up to 3 cm in any direction.

  • Delay alone costs a little. The natural-gait policy, never trained with a delay, goes from 0% to 0.4–1.4% falls at 0–6 cm.
  • Realism training wins where the task lives. At 1 inch and at 4 cm, both retrained policies fall in 0.4% of walks or fewer, and the height-scan one never falls at 0–1 cm.
  • Both retrained policies break down above 6 cm. My leading explanation is the torque cap: with motors less than half as strong, a toe caught on an 8 cm block can't be recovered in time. I haven't isolated it; that needs one more run with the delay but 300 Nm motors.

Realism also slows learning. With the delay and weaker motors, episodes reach 18 seconds at iteration 600 instead of 400, and the curriculum climbs about 200 iterations later. Both runs end at the same level.

A sim-to-sim bug worth knowing about

Isaac Sim's RTX renderer crashed on the L4, so I rendered every video in a second physics engine, Newton (MuJoCo Warp). In the first video the policy fell every time: 512 of 512 robots, within a second. PhysX lists the G1's 37 joints breadth-first; Newton lists them depth-first, leg by leg. The policy was reading its knee angle where it expected a hip. Permuting observations and actions by joint name fixed it with no retraining: 0 of 512 falls in both engines, 14.8 m versus 14.9 m walked. Moving a policy to a real robot's SDK is the same exercise, with less forgiving consequences.

What I'd do next

  • Separate the torque cap from the delay to confirm what breaks the realism policies at 8 cm.
  • Add sensor noise and latency on the height scan, and train with the stairs and slopes a real room also has.
  • Deploy on hardware: the joint-order check, motor delay and torque limits are the pieces of the sim-to-real gap this run already covers.
