Sensor fusion from scratch: tuning an IMU + camera + LiDAR Kalman filter in simulation

I wrote an error-state Kalman filter for an IMU, a camera and a LiDAR in about 250 lines of numpy. Then I spent ~1,000 Monte Carlo runs breaking it the ways real rigs break: a mistuned Q and R, wrong feature matches, 30 ms of latency, an unknown camera clock offset, a wrong camera mount, and a camera that goes dark. In each case, the fix that worked was one you can check without ground truth.

My robot perception work has mostly been end-to-end: policies that map pixels straight to actions. This post is the other half: classical state estimation, built from first principles and tested where I can see the truth. Everything runs in a simulator, so every error is known exactly, and every number below is a mean or median over seeded runs (8 or 16 per setting).

  • Fusion is not optional. Integrating the IMU alone, the position error is 1.3 m after 5 s and 330 m after a minute. With camera updates it stays around 3–5 mm.
  • Tune R with the innovations, then Q with a reference. Position RMSE depends almost entirely on the ratio of Q to R, so ground-truth error can't pin the absolute scale. The normalized innovation squared (NIS) can, and it needs no ground truth. Datasheet noise values gave nearly the best RMSE (+4%), but a covariance about 4× too small.
  • An outlier gate can kill a healthy filter. A 99% χ² gate removed wrong feature matches from 14 of 16 runs, but locked out and diverged on the other 2, even with zero outliers. A five-line guard (if most of a frame fails the gate, blame the filter, not the features) fixed it.
  • Fuse late data at its own timestamp. Fusing 30 ms-old camera frames as if they were current: 57 mm error. Rewinding to the frame's timestamp and replaying the IMU: 4.0 mm, for 1.4× the compute.
  • The filter can estimate its own camera clock offset to within about 1 ms. It also found a 1.25 ms lag in my own IMU integration, which I hadn't put there on purpose.
  • Online extrinsic calibration needs motion. With 3D motion, the camera mount converged to about 1 mm and 0.01°. With planar, yaw-only motion it didn't converge, and the filter was 5× overconfident about it. That made the pose estimate worse than not calibrating at all.

The rig and the room

A handheld-style rig moves on a 60 s figure-eight through a 10 × 10 × 3 m room. It bobs up and down, rolls ±17°, pitches ±14° and turns continuously in yaw, with a peak speed of 1.3 m/s. The room has 300 known landmarks on its walls, floor and ceiling. The trajectory is analytic (sums of sinusoids in position and Euler angles), so the true acceleration and angular rate are exact, and the IMU is simulated from them.

The LiDAR returns matched landmark points instead of a scan to register. Landmark positions are known, so this is map-based localization, not SLAM. None of that changes the estimation problems below, but on a real rig, feature tracking and scan matching are where much of the work is.

The filter

An error-state Kalman filter (ESKF), following Solà's notes. The nominal state is integrated directly from the IMU. The filter tracks a small error state around it, with orientation error as a 3-vector in the body frame, so there are no quaternion constraints inside the Kalman algebra:

nominal  p, v (world)   R (body→world)   b_a, b_g   R_bc, p_bc (camera mount)   t_d (camera clock offset)
error    δp δv δθ δb_a δb_g δφ δp_bc δt_d   — 22 states; ones I'm not estimating get zero covariance

Propagate at every IMU sample (Δt = 2.5 ms), with a = R(a_m − b_a) + g and ω = ω_m − b_g:

p ← p + vΔt + ½aΔt²      v ← v + aΔt      R ← R·Exp(ωΔt)
F:  δp' = δp + δv·Δt
    δv' = δv − R[a_m − b_a]× δθ·Δt − R·δb_a·Δt
    δθ' = Exp(ωΔt)ᵀ δθ − δb_g·Δt
P ← F P Fᵀ + Q·Δt        (Q from the IMU noise densities and bias random walks)

Camera update. A landmark l goes into the body frame, then the camera frame, then the pinhole projection:

p_b = Rᵀ(l − p)      p_c = R_bcᵀ(p_b − p_bc) = (X, Y, Z)      z = (f_x X/Z + c_x,  f_y Y/Z + c_y)

J_π = [f_x/Z  0  −f_x X/Z²;  0  f_y/Z  −f_y Y/Z²]
∂z/∂δp   = −J_π R_bcᵀ Rᵀ          ∂z/∂δθ    = J_π R_bcᵀ [p_b]×
∂z/∂δφ   =  J_π [p_c]×            ∂z/∂δp_bc = −J_π R_bcᵀ
∂z/∂δt_d =  J_π R_bcᵀ (−ω × p_b − Rᵀv)        (how fast the pixel moves)

I checked every Jacobian against finite differences before trusting any result (largest mismatch 4×10⁻⁵ px). The update uses the Joseph form, all features in a frame are stacked into one update, and after each update the error is injected into the nominal state (R ← R·Exp(δθ)) and reset to zero.

The first real bug. With the camera mount added to the state, the filter converged for 0.4 s and then blew up to kilometres. The Jacobians were right. The problem was the covariance: camera updates constrain the camera's pose, so the body position and the mount offset become almost perfectly correlated. KH was then badly conditioned, and the Joseph form, symmetric only in exact arithmetic, drifted to an asymmetric P with a negative variance (−0.03) within ~12 updates. Symmetrizing after every update, P = ½(P + Pᵀ), fixed it. The tell was in P, not in the state: always log the smallest eigenvalue of P.

Warm-up: a complementary filter, and why τ is a tradeoff

The classic attitude-only filter: integrate the gyro, and slowly pull roll and pitch toward the gravity direction measured by the accelerometer, with time constant τ. The accelerometer only measures gravity when the rig isn't accelerating, so a small τ trusts something that's wrong whenever it moves. A large τ lets gyro bias drift build up.

Tuning Q and R

To make tuning a real problem, the simulated sensors are worse than their datasheets: IMU noise 2× the datasheet value (vibration), and features at 1.5 px instead of 1 px. The filter is told the datasheet values times a Q scale and an R scale, and I swept a 7 × 7 grid with 8 runs per cell. Three ways to score each cell:

  • Position RMSE: needs ground truth.
  • ANEES (average normalized estimation error squared, divided by its 15 degrees of freedom): is the filter's covariance honest about its error? 1 is consistent, above 1 overconfident. Also needs ground truth.
  • ANIS (the same test on the camera innovations, divided by 2): needs no ground truth. You can compute it on a real robot.
  1. RMSE is a valley along the diagonal. Scaling Q and R together leaves the Kalman gain, and so the estimate, almost unchanged. RMSE only sees the ratio. The datasheet tuning sits in the valley at 5.9 mm, against 5.6 mm at the true values. The best RMSE cell (5.57 mm) is ×16 / ×8, which is badly wrong in absolute terms.
  2. But the datasheet tuning is 4× overconfident (ANEES 3.9). The estimate is fine and its covariance is lying. This matters as soon as anything reads P: outlier gates, sensor-fusion weights, a planner's safety margin.
  3. ANIS pins R without ground truth. When the filter is tight, camera innovations are mostly pixel noise, so ANIS mostly tracks the R scale: 2.3 at ×1, 1.03 at ×1.5 (the truth), 0.58 at ×2, almost independent of Q.
  4. Then Q needs a reference. With R at ×1.5, RMSE picks Q ×2, the true value (5.6 mm against 6.5 at ×1 and 6.8 at ×8), and ANEES there is 1.18. On hardware that reference is a motion-capture session or a loop of known geometry. Innovation whiteness tests are the no-ground-truth alternative I haven't tried here.

Wrong matches, the χ² gate, and gate lock-out

Wrong data association (a feature matched to the wrong landmark) is the realistic outlier. I replaced a fraction of features with random pixels. The standard defence is to gate each feature on its own innovation: reject it if rᵀS⁻¹r exceeds the 99% χ² value for 2 degrees of freedom (9.21).

The gate alone lost 2 of 16 runs with no outliers at all. In one of them (seed 4) the initial error, a 2–3σ draw of 0.8 m and 8°, made the first frames' innovations larger than the linearized S predicted. The gate rejected most features, the few it accepted pulled the filter toward a confident wrong pose, and from then on it rejected everything and drifted on the IMU. This is gate lock-out, and it's the failure mode of every innovation gate: the filter decides what counts as an outlier using its own estimate.

The guard: if more than half of a frame's features fail the gate, assume the filter is wrong, inflate the pose and velocity covariance by 10×, and re-gate, up to 3 times. That fixed both runs (seed 4: 3.5 mm RMSE, after 3 inflations). It lost no runs up to 20% outliers, where the median error was 4.1 mm against 3.6 mm with no outliers. At 30% it starts to fire on frames that are genuinely mostly wrong (1,101 inflations over 16 runs), let 119 bad matches through, and lost 1 run. The threshold trades lock-out against letting outliers in. A real system would use RANSAC on each frame before the filter sees it.

Latency: rewind and replay

Camera frames arrive late: exposure, readout, transfer and feature extraction easily add 30 ms. The naive filter fuses each frame when it arrives, as if it were current. The fix keeps a 0.3 s ring buffer of checkpoints (state and covariance at each IMU sample) and of recent measurements. When a frame arrives, the filter restores the checkpoint just before the frame's timestamp, propagates to that exact time, applies the update and any later measurements in time order, and re-propagates through the buffered IMU samples to now. The same mechanism handles sensors that arrive out of order.

scenariofuse on arrivalrewind + replayreplay compute

The compute cost is the replayed IMU steps: 12 per frame at 30 ms of latency, 40 at 100 ms. On an embedded target, the cheaper alternatives are to replay only the nominal state and keep the covariance at the measurement time (Larsen's method), or to clone the state at capture time (stochastic cloning).

An unknown camera clock offset

Latency you can measure; a clock offset you often can't. Here the camera's timestamps are wrong by t_d (−30 to +30 ms) on top of 50 ms of latency, and the filter either ignores t_d or estimates it as a state. The measurement Jacobian for t_d is the pixel's velocity: a feature moving fast across the image tells you the most about timing. Two details were needed. Estimation starts after 2 s, once the pose has converged; starting at t = 0 diverged in the runs with a large initial error. And because a frame's fusion time is fixed when it arrives while t_d keeps changing, each replay corrects the predicted pixel to first order for the difference.

The filter found a bug in my integrator. With a true offset of zero, it estimated +1.13 ms in every run. My propagation holds each IMU sample constant over the next 2.5 ms (zero-order hold). The integrated trajectory therefore lags the true one by half a sample, 1.25 ms, and the camera looks 1.25 ms early relative to it. At 800 Hz the estimate halves to +0.58 ms, as a half-sample lag should. Midpoint integration would remove it. Beyond that constant, the estimates are within 0.5 ms of the truth, but the filter's 1σ of 0.1 ms is overconfident for the ±30 ms cases. Those runs linearized around a timing 30 ms off during the first seconds and kept a little of that.

Online camera–IMU extrinsic calibration

The filter starts with the camera mount wrong by 2° about each axis and by (3, −3, 2) cm, and either trusts that or estimates the mount as part of the state. Run on the 3D trajectory, and on a ground robot's trajectory: a constant-height circle with yaw-only rotation.

planar, yaw onlyestimated94 mm7 / 66 / 36 mm21 / 23 / 22 mm1.4–1.8° per axis (1σ 0.25–0.36°)

The reason is observability. The accelerometer sees the lever arm only through how it moves when the rig rotates (R(t)·p_bc has to agree with the measured accelerations). Rotate about one axis and the lever-arm component along that axis never moves, so nothing can measure it. Here that's z, and the error in it stayed at its starting 2–4 cm. Worse, y, which is observable in principle, drifted from 30 to 66 mm, because the filter's correlations along the unobservable directions pulled it off. On planar motion, estimating the mount was worse than leaving it wrong (94 mm against 47 mm). The rotation estimate is also 5× overconfident (error 1.4–1.8°, 1σ 0.3°). That's the known EKF inconsistency: linearizing at the current estimate lets the filter gain spurious information along directions that aren't observable. The practical rule is to calibrate on a deliberate figure-eight with rotation about all three axes, then freeze the mount, or estimate it only while the motion excites it.

Losing the camera: what LiDAR buys

The camera goes dark from 20 to 30 s (lights off, lens blocked). Camera-only, the filter coasts on the IMU. With a 10 Hz LiDAR also in the filter, it keeps getting position fixes.

What I'd take to a real rig

  • Log the consistency statistics, not just the error. NIS per sensor at run time, NEES whenever there's ground truth, and the smallest eigenvalue of P. Most of the failures above showed up there first.
  • Timestamp at capture and fuse at the timestamp. 30 ms of latency fused naively cost 14× in position error. Hardware-trigger or PTP-sync the sensors where possible, and estimate the residual offset.
  • Gate, but protect the gate: RANSAC before the filter, χ² in it, and a lock-out guard around it.
  • Calibrate with motion that excites what you're calibrating, then freeze it. Online calibration during degenerate motion is worse than none.
  • In C++ on a real-time target, the same filter is fixed-size Eigen matrices with no allocation in the loop. IMU propagation runs in the high-rate thread, and the checkpoint buffer is a preallocated ring. Updates run in a lower-priority thread and replay into the ring under a lock, or in a single thread that services the IMU queue first.

Caveats. This is simulation with known data association, Gaussian sensor noise, a rigid rig, global-shutter cameras and a perfectly known map. Real rigs add rolling shutter, motion blur, IMU vibration that isn't white, temperature-dependent biases, and the feature tracking and scan matching this post skips. The numbers show the shape of each tradeoff, not what a specific product would achieve.
