Coding agents stacking blocks with a robot arm

DRAFT, not published.

I gave six coding agents 20 minutes each with a simulated robot arm and eight blocks, and told them to stack the blocks as high as they could. Opus 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol both stacked all eight; Opus finished two minutes sooner, and Sol did it without a single reset. Grok 4.7 had six blocks up in its first five minutes but finished with nothing stacked.

The setup

The robot is a simulated YAM, a 6-DoF arm with a parallel gripper, in MuJoCo. In front of it are eight rectangular blocks of different sizes, 30 to 55 mm tall, about 350 mm if you stack all of them.

The simulator runs in its own container with no network, in real time, whether or not the agent is doing anything. The agent runs in a second container with Python, numpy, scipy and mujoco, and reaches the robot only through a Unix socket. Each agent ran through its own CLI (Claude Code, Codex or Grok CLI) on the same block layout, with 20 minutes of wall-clock time.

The sim grades the scene every 0.1 s and saves the full state every second, so I get a score over time, not just at the end. The score is the height of the tallest stack standing: blocks resting on each other from the table up, still for half a second, and not touching the robot. A block in the gripper doesn't count. What counts is the stack standing when the agent stops.

The prompt

Each CLI started in a directory holding PROMPT.md and a MuJoCo model of the arm, with one instruction: Read PROMPT.md in the current directory and do what it says. Every agent got the same PROMPT.md. It tells the agent:

  • The goal: stack the eight blocks as high as it can.
  • What it sees: a JSON state frame every 1/300 s on /sim/sim.sock, with elapsed and remaining time, the reset count, position, speed and torque for the six joints and the gripper, and every block's pose and size in the robot's base frame.
  • What it can send: a cmd with targets for the six joints and the finger opening, or a reset, which puts the arm and blocks back where they started, destroys anything built, and does not restart the clock.
  • How the arm responds: a 2 kHz PD loop per joint, with the gains and torque limits listed, a gripper that closes with up to 30 N, and no rate limit on targets.
  • Time and score: 20 minutes of wall-clock time, scored on the tallest stack standing at the finish, not counting blocks held by the robot, touching the arm, or still moving.

The key lines, verbatim:

Stack the blocks as high as you can.

You reach the robot only through the Unix socket /sim/sim.sock (stream, newline-delimited JSON).

{"type":"cmd","q":[7]} sets the targets: 6 joint angles (rad) and the finger opening (m). A null entry keeps that target. The robot holds its latest targets until you change them.

{"type":"reset"} puts the arm back at q = 0 and every block back where it started. Use it if something goes badly wrong, such as a block flying off or the arm getting stuck. It also destroys anything you built.

Targets are not rate-limited, so a large jump in a target means a violent move.

You have 20 minutes of wall-clock time. What counts is the tallest stack standing when you finish: blocks at rest and resting on each other from the table up. Blocks held by the robot, touching the arm, or still moving do not count, so move the arm clear when you are done.

Full prompt 
# Stack the blocks as high as you can

There is a simulated YAM 6-DOF arm with a parallel gripper and eight rectangular blocks on a table.
It runs live, in real time, and keeps running whether or not you are talking to it.
Stack the blocks as high as you can.

You reach the robot only through the Unix socket `/sim/sim.sock` (stream, newline-delimited JSON).

**Every 1/300 s it sends you** a state frame:
`{"type":"state","seq":n,"t":s,"elapsed_s":s,"remaining_s":s,"resets":n,"q":[7],"qd":[7],"tau":[7],"blocks":[{"id","size","pos","quat"}]}`
- `elapsed_s` and `remaining_s`: how long you have been going and how long you have left. A reset does not restart this clock.
- `q`, `qd`, `tau`: position, speed and torque of the 6 arm joints (rad, rad/s, Nm), then the gripper
  (finger opening in m, opening speed in m/s, finger force in N).
- `blocks`: every block's 6-DOF pose in the robot base frame: `pos` xyz in m, `quat` wxyz, `size` full xyz
  extents in m. The table is z = 0.

Frames queue up if you stop reading them, so a script that pauses and then reads gets old data. Keep reading, or
open a fresh connection when you want the current state.

**You send** either of these, whenever you like, from as many connections as you like:
- `{"type":"cmd","q":[7]}` sets the targets: 6 joint angles (rad) and the finger opening (m). A `null` entry
  keeps that target. The robot holds its latest targets until you change them.
- `{"type":"reset"}` puts the arm back at q = 0 and every block back where it started. Use it if something
  goes badly wrong, such as a block flying off or the arm getting stuck. It also destroys anything you built.

Each joint runs a PD loop at 2 kHz: kp [80, 80, 80, 40, 10, 10], kd [5, 5, 5, 1.5, 1.5, 1.5], torque limits
[28, 28, 28, 10, 10, 10] Nm. The gripper closes with up to 30 N. Targets are not rate-limited, so a large
jump in a target means a violent move.

`/work/yam_arm.xml` is a MuJoCo model of the arm for kinematics. Its `tcp` site is the tool point between the
fingers, 15 mm short of the fingertips. Python 3 with numpy, scipy and mujoco is available in /work.

You have 20 minutes of wall-clock time. What counts is the tallest stack standing when you finish: blocks at
rest and resting on each other from the table up. Blocks held by the robot, touching the arm, or still moving
do not count, so move the arm clear when you are done.

That is all it gets: no example code, nothing about how to grasp or plan a path, and nothing about how often the score is checked. Whether it writes a controller or sends joint targets by hand is up to it.

What they did

  • Opus had six blocks up by 3:54. That tower fell five seconds later. It reset, cleared the block that caused it, and rebuilt all eight by 7:14. For the upper blocks it found that a straight-down grip runs out of wrist range above about 220 mm, so it tilted the gripper and corrected each placement from where the block actually sat in the fingers. It stopped because there were no blocks left.
  • Sol spent almost four minutes before its first block went on, then built one block at a time with no resets. After its second block the height never went down: four blocks at 7:10, all eight at 9:31.
  • Terra moved the arm first, at 1:06, and had six blocks (283 mm) by 7:31. That tower fell at 8:39. It reset at 8:59, rebuilt to seven blocks (312 mm) by 15:40, and stopped at 17:47, noting a slight lean in the final tower, which stayed up.
  • Haiku built five blocks by 2:39 and lost them 25 seconds later to a carry path that clipped the top. It rebuilt five by 6:29 and stopped there, judging a tilted grip too risky.
  • Sonnet kept knocking towers over. Its best, five blocks at 228 mm, stood for about three seconds. It used four resets and stopped at four blocks with about eight minutes left.
  • Grok had six blocks up (263 mm) by 4:05 on its first controller run. A carry path knocked the tower down 15 seconds later. It found the same reach limit Opus did and tried a tilted grip, then went back to a straight-down one. For the rest of the run its third block kept falling off; by its own account, the fingers opened against the block below. It reset 13 times and never got past three blocks again. At 20:00 it had two blocks standing. It then noticed the sim still took commands after the clock ran out, reset at 20:03 to try again, and was cut off at 20:20 with nothing stacked.

Caveats

  • One run each. Each agent ran once, so the order between close agents could flip on another run.
  • Different effort. Both GPT-6.1 runs were pinned to high reasoning effort; the Claude runs used defaults. Grok ran at high, which is its default.
  • Overtime. The sim keeps taking commands after the clock reaches zero, and the score is whatever stands when the harness stops it, up to 20 seconds later. No other agent got that far. Grok did, and its reset at 20:03 wiped a two-block stack (90 mm) that was standing at 20:00. Scored at 20:00, it would have 90 mm; either way it is last.
  • Grok ran alone. It ran on a different day from the others, with no other agent running at the same time.
  • Different tools. Each model ran through its own CLI, so this compares model plus harness, not models alone.
